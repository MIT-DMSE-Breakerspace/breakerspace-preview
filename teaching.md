## Collaboration Pathways {#collaboration-pathways}

Different courses need different levels of structure. These are common ways instructors can work with the Breakerspace.

### Course Orientation

A course orientation introduces students to the Breakerspace, the available instruments, the lounge and lab layout, and the basic rules for access, safety, scheduling, and data handling. This is a good fit when students may use the lab later for projects but do not all need the same instrument workflow.

3.001 has used this model consistently over several years, typically visiting the lab for one or two exploration sessions each semester.

Useful when:

* Students need to know what the Breakerspace makes possible.
* A course includes open-ended projects.
* The teaching team wants students to discover appropriate instruments rather than assign one fixed method.

### Instrument Training For A Course Cohort

Students complete Breakerspace training for one or more instruments that are central to the course. After training, students can reserve trained-user time according to the normal Breakerspace access model.

Subject instructors, teaching assistants, and teaching fellows can also be trained to lead instrument activities and help manage teaching in the lab. 3.040 is an established example of this instructor-led model.

Useful when:

* Students need hands-on experience, not only demonstrations.
* A recurring lab or project requires independent instrument use.
* The course can prepare students with a shared sample, protocol, or learning goal before training.

### Guided Lab Module

A guided module is a structured activity in which students use one or more instruments to answer a focused question. The strongest version is usually co-taught: the subject teaching team connects the activity to the course while Breakerspace staff guide instrument use and lab practice. Breakerspace staff can also serve as the sole in-lab instructors for a well-scoped session.

Useful when:

* The course needs a repeatable lab activity.
* Students are new to characterization tools.
* The main learning goal is connecting material behavior, instrument output, and interpretation.

### Project Support

For project-based subjects, the Breakerspace can help students choose feasible measurements, prepare samples appropriately, collect data, and understand what the results can and cannot show.

3.042 shows what project support can look like at the advanced end. Because it is a capstone subject, students ideally enter already trained and able to work independently. Their Breakerspace use is informal rather than a scheduled course engagement, with staff helping students build additional capability when their projects require more advanced characterization.

Useful when:

* Students bring diverse project ideas.
* The course emphasizes experimental design.
* Teams need help turning broad curiosity into a measurable question.

#### Getting Help With Advanced Work

Already-trained students who encounter an advanced characterization question should:

1. Ask a Breakerspace staff member in the lab, if someone is available.
2. If no one is available, ask in `#breakerspace-help` on the [Breakerspace Slack workspace](https://mit-dmse-breakerspace.slack.com) or email [dmse-breakerspace@mit.edu](mailto:dmse-breakerspace@mit.edu), whichever they prefer.

The Breakerspace team can help students identify a feasible approach, develop additional instrument skills, and decide whether the work requires more preparation or training.

### Course-Integrated Rotation

A rotation uses several instruments or workflows in sequence. Students may rotate through stations such as microscopy, spectroscopy, mechanical testing, particle sizing, or sample preparation, then combine the evidence into a larger interpretation.

Useful when:

* The teaching goal is comparison across methods.
* The class can be split into small teams.
* Students should see how different instruments answer different kinds of questions.

### Breakerspace-Centered Subject

Some subjects can be built around the Breakerspace as a primary teaching environment. [3.000 Coffee Matters](./3000.html) is one example: students use coffee as a familiar materials system, moving between tasting, brewing, microscopy, spectroscopy, particle sizing, hardness testing, and project work.

Useful when:

* The subject is designed around hands-on exploration.
* The material system is safe, familiar, and rich enough for real measurement.
* Students can collect, compare, and explain data over several weeks.

## What Breakerspace Teaching Works Especially Well For

The Breakerspace is strongest when students are asked to connect what they can see, touch, measure, and reason about.

Good teaching uses include:

* Helping students turn curiosity into testable materials questions.
* Showing how processing changes structure, chemistry, performance, or appearance.
* Comparing different measurement techniques on the same sample.
* Teaching students to choose an instrument based on the question, not the novelty of the tool.
* Giving first-year and early undergraduate students an accessible first experience with research-style instrumentation.
* Supporting project-based learning, design subjects, discovery subjects, and lab modules where the result is not known in advance.
* Teaching the limits of data: resolution, sampling, artifacts, preparation effects, uncertainty, and interpretation.

## Possible Module Themes

| Theme | Possible instruments | Student output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surface structure and texture | Optical microscope, SEM, ion mill | Annotated images, before/after comparisons, scale-aware observations |
| Chemical fingerprints | FTIR, Raman, UV-Vis | Spectra, peak labels, material comparisons, limits of identification |
| Crystals and phases | XRD | Diffraction patterns, phase matches, discussion of unknown or extra peaks |
| Mechanical behavior | Instron, hardness tester | Stress-strain curves, hardness values, fracture observations |
| Powders and particles | Particle size analyzer, optical microscope, SEM | Size distributions, image comparisons, preparation notes |
| Sample preparation effects | Ion mill, SEM, optical microscope | Before/after images, artifact recognition, preparation recommendations |
| Everyday materials as systems | Multiple instruments | A short evidence-based explanation linking structure, processing, and behavior |

## Designing A Course Experience

The most successful course integrations usually begin with a specific teaching goal rather than a specific instrument. A good planning conversation often starts with these questions:

1. What should students understand or be able to do after the activity?
2. What material, object, or sample will make the question feel real?
3. What evidence would help students answer the question?
4. Which instrument can produce that evidence at the right level of complexity?
5. What should students produce: images, spectra, plots, a short memo, a lab report, a presentation, or a project proposal?
6. What preparation, training, staffing, and scheduling are needed for the class size?

For many undergraduate courses, the best activity is not the most technically advanced measurement. It is the measurement students can connect to a clear question, perform safely, and interpret honestly.

## How The Work Is Shared

Good course collaborations work best when the teaching team owns the academic goals and the Breakerspace team helps translate those goals into safe, feasible lab experiences. Whenever possible, both teams should be present in the lab: subject instructors, teaching assistants, and teaching fellows bring the course context, while Breakerspace staff bring instrument and lab expertise.

Teaching assistants and teaching fellows are especially valuable partners because they often help develop the exercise and teach at the instruments during lab sessions. With enough advance notice, the Breakerspace team can work with them before the course visit to build the training and expertise they will need.

| The Breakerspace team can help with... | The teaching team should bring... |
| --- | --- |
| Matching learning goals to instruments and workflows. | Course context, learning goals, and what students have already learned. |
| Reviewing sample compatibility and preparation needs. | Sample information, expected hazards or handling concerns, and any constraints on materials. |
| Advising on training, reservations, staffing, and lab logistics. | Enrollment, schedule constraints, section structure, and teaching-team availability, including who can train in advance. |
| Creating or adapting instrument-facing instructions, sample kits, or shared datasets. | Student-facing assignment goals, grading expectations, and course deliverables. |
| Helping students understand what the data can and cannot show. | The conceptual framing that connects the lab activity back to the course. |

### Who Can Lead A Session

| Teaching model | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Shared instruction | The preferred model. Subject instructors, teaching assistants, teaching fellows, and Breakerspace staff teach together, dividing attention between course concepts, instrument operation, and student questions. |
| Breakerspace-led instruction | Breakerspace staff serve as the in-lab instructors for a defined activity coordinated with the teaching team. |
| Teaching team-led instruction | Trained subject instructors, teaching assistants, or teaching fellows manage the teaching in the lab. 3.040 is an established example of this model. |

## Planning Rhythm

| Type of engagement | When to contact the Breakerspace |
| --- | --- |
| Short orientation, exploration session, or other low-lift visit | At least one to two weeks ahead when possible, subject to staff and lab availability. |
| Designed lab activity, coordinated instrument training, repeated lab use, or a large course cohort | Before the semester begins whenever possible, so both teams can align learning goals, staffing, advance training for the teaching team, samples, and schedules. |

A typical course collaboration then moves through five stages:

1. Explore the idea and identify the learning goal.
2. Choose a safe material system, sample set, or project scope.
3. Match the activity to instruments, training, staffing, and reservation needs.
4. Prepare student instructions, sample logistics, data-handling plans, and assessment expectations.
5. Run the activity, then debrief what worked and what should change for the next offering.

For new or ambitious collaborations, starting before the syllabus and lab schedule are final makes it easier to choose the right model and build the experience into the course from the beginning.

## Student Preparation

Students get more from Breakerspace time when they arrive with a small amount of shared preparation.

Before the lab, consider giving students:

* A one-paragraph statement of the question they are investigating.
* A short description of the sample and why it is safe to use.
* A few vocabulary terms they will see on the instrument page.
* A prediction prompt: what do they expect to observe or measure?
* A data plan: where files should be saved, how files should be named, and what information must be recorded.

During the lab, students benefit from:

* Small teams with defined roles.
* A concise checklist for the instrument or station.
* Prompts that ask students to observe first, then measure.
* A required note about instrument settings, sample preparation, and anything that went wrong.

After the lab, students should be asked to explain:

* What the instrument actually measured.
* What the data suggest.
* What the data do not prove.
* What additional measurement would make the conclusion stronger.

## Access, Safety, And Scheduling

Breakerspace instruments are available to trained users, and course activities should be planned with training, staffing, sample safety, and reservation needs in mind. Review [Safety And Lab Use]({{ "/safety.html" | relative_url }}) when developing a lab activity or preparing a teaching team.

Important constraints:

* Students must complete the required training before using instruments independently.
* Course groups should coordinate with Breakerspace staff before assuming instrument availability.
* One to two weeks' notice is appropriate for many brief visits, but does not guarantee a particular date, instrument, or staffing arrangement.
* Sessions work best with no more than five or six students at one instrument at a time.
* Larger classes should rotate through in smaller groups or use several instruments in parallel.
* Parallel instrument sessions work best with one trained instructor, teaching assistant, or teaching fellow assigned to each active instrument.
* Materials should be non-hazardous and safe to handle in the Breakerspace.
* Unusual samples should be discussed before they are brought to the lab, especially if they are wet, loose, reactive, biological, vacuum-sensitive, magnetic, odorous, sharp, fragile, unusually large, or unknown.
* Students should follow the cleanup, file-saving, and shutdown instructions on the relevant instrument page.

The Breakerspace team can help decide whether a course activity should use independent student reservations, guided sessions, shared datasets, prepared sample kits, or a hybrid model.

## What To Include When You Reach Out

When contacting the Breakerspace team about a course idea, include as much of the following as you can:

* Course number and title.
* Term or target date.
* Estimated enrollment.
* Whether the course is lecture-based, lab-based, project-based, design-focused, or discovery-focused.
* Learning goals for the activity.
* Instruments, samples, or materials you already have in mind.
* Whether students need independent access or a guided class session.
* Which instructors, teaching assistants, or teaching fellows could prepare in advance and help teach in the lab.
* Any constraints on schedule, classroom time, staffing, deliverables, or assessment.

It is fine if some of these details are still uncertain. The Breakerspace team can help shape an activity from an early idea.

## Established Course Models {#established-course-models}

Breakerspace already supports teaching at several levels of engagement. These examples can be useful starting points, but they are not the limits of what a collaboration can become.

### 3.000 Coffee Matters

[3.000 Coffee Matters](./3000.html) uses coffee as a familiar entry point into materials science. Students brew, taste, measure, image, and analyze coffee while learning how structure, processing, chemistry, and instrumentation connect to the final cup.

The course works because coffee is approachable but scientifically rich. Students can taste a difference, change a brewing variable, collect data, and ask whether the measurement explains what they experienced. That same model can be adapted to other safe, familiar material systems when the teaching goal is to connect observation, measurement, and interpretation.

### 3.001 Exploration Sessions

3.001 has visited the Breakerspace consistently over several years for one or two exploration sessions per semester. This lighter-touch model gives students meaningful exposure to the lab and its instruments without requiring a full course integration.

### 3.010 Guided XRD Exercise And Training

3.010 students take part in a course-integrated [XRD](./instruments/xrd.html) exercise and instrument training in the Breakerspace. This guided-module model connects diffraction concepts from the subject to sample handling, measurement, and interpretation in the lab.

### 3.042 Project Support

3.042 is a capstone subject whose students ideally arrive already trained and independent in the Breakerspace. There is no formal course engagement with the lab, but students use it extensively as their projects develop. Their advanced characterization needs often lead to informal collaboration: Breakerspace staff help students extend their abilities, while the students' projects help the lab develop and refine its own capabilities.

### 3.040 Instructor-Led Teaching

In 3.040, trained members of the subject teaching team fully manage their teaching in the Breakerspace. This model can work well when a teaching team wants recurring or specialized lab use and is prepared to develop the necessary instrument and lab expertise.
